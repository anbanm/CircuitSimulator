# NOBODY WAS SENT

*A novella*

---

*This is fiction. Secunda is a real town. The colliery, the security firm, the guesthouse, the radio station, the building called Atlas House and every person in these pages are invented, and none of them is a portrait of anyone living or dead. The plant is only ever the skyline.*

---

## PART ONE: THE SMELL

### 1

Ruth Mahlangu came into Secunda on a Monday evening in September with the windows up, and the smell came in anyway.

It was not a bad smell at first. It was a struck match, a boiled egg left in a lunch tin, the inside of a geyser cupboard. It arrived somewhere after the turnoff and it sat in the car beside her like a hitchhiker who was not going to make conversation.

On the horizon the plant was coming on for the night. There were cooling towers the colour of old teeth, and steam going up out of them and leaning east. There was a chimney so tall that it had red lights on it to warn aeroplanes, which did not come here. There were two flames on the ends of two pipes, burning off whatever the plant could not use. They had been burning since before she was born and they would be burning after everyone she was about to meet was dead.

Some of them were going to be dead quite soon. One of them already was.

A conveyor came in across the veld on steel legs, kilometre after kilometre of it, carrying coal to the plant the way a drip carries sugar water to a vein. Under it the grass was the brown of the end of winter. The firebreaks had been burned along the fences, and the black strips ran beside the road like something a child had done with a ruler.

She had a room booked at a guesthouse and a client she had never met. The client was a man named Daniel Brandt, who was thirty-six and ran a radio station that was mostly not on the radio. He had phoned her office in Johannesburg four times. He had a voice like someone leaning on a doorbell. He had said: *I have something I need looked at by a person who doesn't live here.* He had said he would pay cash and that he would explain when she arrived.

He would not explain. He had been dead for forty minutes.

Nobody had told Ruth, so she drove on toward him at the speed limit, a careful woman in a grey hatchback with a pistol under her blazer that she had drawn twice in her life, into a town with twenty thousand strangers in it.

### 2

The strangers were there for the shutdown.

Once a year the plant was switched off, one piece at a time, so that men could climb inside it and scrape it and weld it and put it back. The plant could not do this for itself. So for three or four weeks every September, contractors came from everywhere: boilermakers from Durban, scaffolders from Sasolburg, pipe fitters from Mozambique who slept six to a room and sent everything home. Twenty thousand of them, more or less. Nobody counted the less.

They filled every guesthouse and every back room and every converted garage. They filled the takeaways and the taverns and the parking lots. They wore overalls with other towns' names on the back. They were all, every one of them, somebody nobody here had seen before, and so for one month a year Secunda was the easiest place in the province to be a stranger.

This matters. Remember it.

The water was also off. It had been off in parts of town for eleven days. The municipality owed a great deal of money to the people who sold it water, and to the people who sold it electricity, and it had been explained to the residents that these things were being addressed. Green plastic tanks stood on stands in the yards of those who could afford them. Those who could not stood in a queue behind a truck.

So: a town with no water, twenty thousand extra men, and a smell. Ruth turned in at a face-brick house with a sign that said TAU'S REST and a yard full of white bakkies with orange lights on their roofs.

### 3

Grace Tau owned the guesthouse. She was sixty and built like a kitchen dresser, and she stood in her back doorway beside a brazier made from a paint tin, feeding it lumps of coal with her fingers.

"You're the last one," she said. "I gave the others away. Room four. There's a bucket in the bath, that's your water, don't wash your hair."

"I'm meeting someone here," Ruth said. "Daniel Brandt."

Grace Tau looked at her for the length of time it takes to count money.

"No you're not," she said.

That was how Ruth found out.

Grace Tau knew because she knew everything. Knowing things was her second business. Her first business was beds, and her third was lending money to people who could not get it from a bank, and holding their bank cards in a biscuit tin until they paid it back. The three businesses fed each other. A man who slept in your bed told you things. A man who owed you money told you more.

She sold what she knew. She did not think of this as wicked. A shop sells bread.

"Atlas House," she said. "On Horwood. You can't miss it, it's the one with everybody standing outside. You want supper, it's seven. It's pap and wors. It's always pap and wors."

### 4

Atlas House was five storeys of brown brick with concrete bands around it, built in 1983 by people who believed the town would need offices. The town had needed some. The ground floor had a lobby, a cash-loan shop and a pharmacy that had closed. Above that it was mostly empty. On the fourth floor there was a music school. On the fifth there was Daniel Brandt's radio station, and on the roof there was his antenna, with a red light on top.

The red light was the only light. The power was out in the middle of town. Somebody had dug up a cable on Saturday night and sold it for the copper, and the municipality had explained that this was being addressed.

So the crowd stood in the dark. There were perhaps sixty of them: shop girls, car guards in yellow bibs, contractors with their boiler suits tied round their waists by the arms, schoolchildren who should have been somewhere else. A police van stood with its blue light going round and round over all of them, and each time it came round it showed the same thing. A paved place outside the lobby doors. A length of tape. A grey blanket with a shape under it, and a pair of shoes.

They were good shoes. He had been a man who liked shoes.

Nobody was crying. They stood the way people stand at the edge of a hole in the road, to see how deep it is.

"Robbery," said a voice at Ruth's shoulder.

The voice belonged to a tired man in a brown jacket with a police lanyard round his neck. He was about fifty. He had a moustache he had clearly stopped thinking about, and a clipboard, and he was writing on the clipboard by the light of his phone.

"Phone's gone," he said. "Laptop bag's gone. People saw him carry it out, now it's not here. So. Robbery. You knew him?"

"He hired me."

"To do what?"

"He didn't say."

The policeman wrote that down. It did not take long.

"Captain Khoza," he said. "Detectives. And you're private." He had seen the shape under her blazer. He did not seem to mind it. "Then you know how this goes, Mma. I've got a hundred and fifty dockets. I've got two cars that start. One of them is that one." He nodded at the van. "A robbery I can close. I put *robbery* and I put *unknown suspects* and it goes in a drawer and it's finished and I can go and look at the next one. You understand?"

"His wallet?"

Khoza stopped writing.

"In his pocket," he said.

"Watch?"

"On his arm."

"Car?"

The car was parked ten metres away, a silver double cab with the keys in the dead man's hand.

Khoza looked at her, and then at the blanket, and then at his clipboard, like a man who has been handed a plate he did not order.

"It's a robbery," he said, gently. "Go and eat something."

### 5

This is what the town said, in the dark, while the blue light went round.

It said: *It's because of the radio.* It said: *He was talking about the mine again.* It said: *His own father.* It said, more quietly: *It's the Forum, it's those truck people, it's Mokoena.* And someone said, quietest of all, the way you would mention the weather to God: *Hit.*

So there were two stories by eight o'clock. The police had a robbery. The town had a hit.

Both stories were wrong. They were wrong in the way people are usually wrong about these things, which is that they were too big. What had happened to Daniel Brandt was small. It would have fitted in a desk drawer. In fact it had come out of one.

But both stories were useful to somebody, and that kept them alive long after they should have died. A robbery was useful to Captain Khoza, who could close it. A hit was useful to certain other people, who were delighted to be suspected of a murder they had not committed, so long as nobody looked at their paperwork.

An old man stood at the back of the crowd with his hat in his hand. He had a grey moustache and a scar across the bridge of his nose that was faintly blue, the way a tattoo is blue. Coal does that if it gets into a cut. He was looking at the shoes.

He saw Ruth looking at him. He put his hat back on and walked away toward the taxi rank. He walked like a man carrying something, although his hands were empty.

His name was Joseph Sithole. In the back room of his house in eMbalenhle, under a blanket, was a wooden crate that Daniel Brandt had asked him to keep. He had not opened it. It had not occurred to him to open it. It was not his.

### 6

At eleven that night the police took the tape away, because they needed it for something else.

Ruth went back.

The lobby doors were chained, but the guard on the desk was asleep with his chin on his vest, and the door to the fire stair on the side of the building had been propped open with a brick by somebody who smoked. She went up five flights in the dark with her phone held low. On the fourth floor she passed a green door with a paper sign on it, a treble clef drawn in koki pen. On the fifth the door stood open.

The radio station was two rooms. One had egg-box foam on the walls and a desk with a microphone on a bent arm. The other had a kettle, a couch with a sleeping bag on it, and a wall of photographs of the plant taken at night, hundreds of them, as if he had been trying to catch it doing something.

Between the photographs he had pinned newspaper cuttings, gone the brown of weak tea. She read the headlines by the light of her phone. *177 DEAD IN KINROSS MINE FIRE.* That was 1986. *53 KILLED IN MIDDELBULT BLAST.* That was 1993. One was older than both, soft as cloth: *435 ENTOMBED AT COALBROOK.* 1960. Someone had gone over the numbers in ballpoint pen, more than once, the way you press on a bruise to see if it is still there.

That made six hundred and sixty-five men. They had all gone to work on a morning much like this one had been. They were under this town and the towns around it, and they were not going anywhere.

She did not touch anything. She looked. Looking was most of what she was.

The desk had three drawers. The bottom one was open. In it was a pistol case, the hard plastic kind with foam cut to the shape of a gun, and the foam was empty. Next to the case was a box of nine-millimetre rounds with one row missing.

So he had owned a gun, and it had lived in this drawer, and it was gone. Whoever took it had known which drawer.

She stood for a while in the dark with that. There was no feeling in it yet. It was only a fact, like a stone in a shoe.

On the desk a small red lamp was lit. It said ON AIR. She thought it was broken. It was not. Down in the basement was a generator the building's owners had forgotten they paid for, and it had come on by itself when the power went. A computer in the corner was still doing what it had been told. It was playing Daniel Brandt's programmes, one after another, out through the antenna on the roof and into the dark, to anybody who was listening.

Nobody had told it he was dead either.

### 7

**RADIO SKOORSTEEN. ARCHIVE. A THURSDAY IN AUGUST, 01:40.**

…and good morning again to the insomniacs, the night shift, the men lying awake in other people's garages doing arithmetic, and to you, sir, yes you, the one in the armed response vehicle parked outside my building with your lights off. I can see you from up here. You look like a fridge with a moustache. Go home.

This is Radio Skoorsteen, the voice of the chimney, broadcasting to a potential audience of forty thousand and an actual audience of, let me check the numbers, yes, eleven. Eleven of you. I love you all. One of you I think is a dog.

I want to talk about the smell.

Everybody who comes here says the same thing: *You get used to it.* They say it the way a man says it about his marriage. And it's true, God help us, you do. You stop smelling it by Christmas. Your nose packs a bag and leaves. And then one morning you drive to Bethal to buy a tractor part, and you come back over the rise, and it hits you fresh, like walking into a room where someone has just said something unforgivable, and you think: *I live in that. I brush my teeth in that. I have been breathing that for twenty-nine years and my body has decided, as a matter of policy, not to mention it.*

That is the thing I want you to understand about this town, my eleven darlings. It is not that anything here is hidden. Nothing here is hidden! It's three hundred metres tall! It's on fire! You can see it from space! No. The trick is not hiding. The trick is *getting used to it.* That is the only industry we have ever truly mastered. We could export it.

So here is tonight's question, and then I will play you some Brenda Fassie and have a small brandy for my nerves. What *else* have you got used to? What is sitting in the room with you right now, stinking, that you stopped noticing in about 2019?

I'll go first. I know something about a coal mine.

Not tonight. I'm a coward tonight. But soon. Stay tuned. Tell the dog.

### 8

The car from the farm came for her at midnight.

It was a white bakkie with a crest on the door, a shield with a sword through it, and under the shield the words STEYN REAKSIE. The driver did not speak. He had a shaved head and he drove with one hand, and at every intersection where the traffic lights were dead he slowed down exactly enough to show that he could have chosen not to.

They went out past the last streetlight and the last precast wall. The road turned to gravel. Blue gums stood along it in a row, pale as bones, planted by somebody's grandfather as a windbreak against a wind that had not taken the hint.

The farmhouse was older than the town. It was older than the plant, and older than the idea of the plant. It had a tin roof and a stoep and a lamp burning in one window. Behind it, where the maize should have been, the ground fell away into a darkness with a few yellow lights in it, and a low grinding sound came up out of that darkness and did not stop. That was the mine. It was called Brandtskraal Colliery. It had been a farm, and then it had been a hole.

Hendrik Brandt was seventy-eight. He sat in a wing chair with a blanket over his knees and a tube under his nose that ran to a machine on the floor. The machine breathed for him in small sighs. On the wall behind him were photographs of men standing in front of machinery, and a framed plan of something underground, drawn in careful coloured inks.

There was a tray of tea. Nobody had touched it.

He looked at her for a long time. He had pale eyes. They had not gone soft, the way old men's eyes do. They had gone the other way.

"You're what my son spent my money on," he said.

"He hadn't paid me yet."

"He wouldn't have. He never paid for anything. He thought things should be free. Water. Electricity. The truth." The machine sighed. "What did he want you for?"

"He said he had something he wanted looked at."

Something moved behind the old man's face and was put away again.

"He had a great many things he wanted looked at," Hendrik Brandt said. "He wanted the whole world looked at. It's a tiring way to live. I am going to pay you what he owed you, and then I am going to pay you the same again, and for that I want one thing."

He lifted one hand off the blanket. It shook, and he looked at it with dislike until it stopped.

"Find who killed my son," he said. "Then go home."

It was the first thing anyone in Secunda had asked her to do, and she did it. That was the joke, if you like jokes. She did exactly what the old man wanted, and it was the worst thing that could have happened to him.

But that was eleven days off. She drank none of his tea. The man with the shaved head drove her back.

### 9

On Tuesday morning she went to see the body.

The mortuary was not in Secunda. It was eight kilometres away in Evander, which was a gold town, or had been. Two headgears still stood up out of the grass on the edge of it, steel towers with wheels at the top for winding men down into the ground. One of the wheels still turned.

The man who cut up the dead for the state in that part of the world was called Zwane. He was small and neat, and he wore a jersey under his plastic apron because the fridges were the only thing in the building that worked properly. He had four bodies waiting. Three of them had been waiting since the weekend.

"A stabbing from the tavern at Extension 14," he said, counting on his fingers. "A woman from Kinross, the boyfriend. And one they say was stealing a television. The community got to him first." He looked at his fingers as if they had let him down. "Yours is the fourth. Yours came with a policeman. The others came in a van."

Nobody was sent for the stabbing. Nobody was sent for the woman from Kinross. As for the man with the television, forty people had killed him, which is the same as no one.

"You're not supposed to be here," Zwane said.

"No."

"The report will say *gunshot wound, chest.* It will be two lines. They don't like long reports. They can't file them." He pulled back the sheet as far as the collarbone and no further, which was a courtesy. "I'll tell you what the two lines won't say. One shot. Nine millimetre, I'd put money. And close. This close." He held his hand a few centimetres from his own chest. "There's burning on the shirt. You don't get that from across a parking lot. You get that from someone who was standing where you're standing. Someone he let stand there."

"Would a robber stand that close?"

"A robber shoots you because you're running away, or running at him." Zwane covered Daniel Brandt's face again, and tucked the sheet in at the side, as you would for a child. "This man wasn't running anywhere. He was having a conversation."

He washed his hands.

"I didn't tell you that," he said. "I've got a pension."

### 10

When she got back to Atlas House the power was on, and the cameras in the lobby had come back to life, too late to have seen anything.

There was also something new on the paving outside the doors.

It lay where the blanket had been, in a ring of chalk, with a little numbered tent beside it. A constable was photographing it. It was a brass cartridge case, long and bottle-shaped, the kind that comes out of a rifle. The kind that comes out of the rifles that are used, in that province, when one taxi association wishes to discuss a route with another.

"Found it this morning," said a man beside her. "Under the flower box. Your police walked past it six times last night. Can you believe that?"

He was a big, pleasant man of fifty in a khaki shirt with the shield and the sword on the pocket. He had a beard trimmed square and a radio on his belt that muttered to itself. He was smiling at her as if they had met at a braai.

"Marius Steyn," he said. "We do the security for this building. For most buildings. You'll be the lady Oom Hendrik's got." He looked down at the cartridge case and shook his head sadly. "Rifle. So. That's not a robbery, is it? That's a message. That's those truck people." He said *truck people* as you might say *rats*. "I've been telling the Captain for a year. They want a piece of everything. Now they want a piece of the Brandts."

Ruth looked at the cartridge case. She thought of a small neat man holding his hand a few centimetres from his chest. Nine millimetre. Close.

A rifle makes a hole you could put a fist in. Daniel Brandt had a hole you could have covered with a coin.

She did not say so. She had been a policewoman for ten years and a private one for nine, and the main thing she had learned in nineteen years was the same thing the town had learned about the smell. You did not have to mention everything you noticed.

"Thank you," she said. "That's very helpful."

"Any time," said Marius Steyn. He meant it. He was a helpful man. He had been up since four, helping.

## PART TWO: THE DESK DRAWER

### 11

She did not dream on Monday night. She lay in room four with the window open a hand's width and the smell came in and sat on her chest, and it brought nothing with it. It is the only smell there is that brings nothing. Bread brings a kitchen. Rain brings a road. That one brings only itself, and the knowledge that you will have stopped noticing it by Christmas.

On Tuesday afternoon, walking back from the mortuary taxi through one of the old suburbs, she passed a peach tree.

The suburbs of Secunda were drawn in 1974 by a Swiss gentleman who believed in walking. He gave each neighbourhood a school that a child could reach on foot and a strip of green to reach it by, and he curled the streets round on themselves so that cars would have to slow down. Then he went home to Switzerland. The houses that were built along his streets were low and the colour of biscuits. Each stood behind a wall of concrete panels, and over the walls came whatever the people inside had planted forty years ago and stopped pruning.

This one was a peach tree. It was in flower. It had put a whole branch out over the wall and across the pavement, pink as the inside of a mouth, and the wind moved it, and a few petals came down on the tar.

She stopped. She could not have said why. Something went soft at the edges for a moment, the way things do when you stand up too fast.

Then a dog barked behind the wall and she walked on. But that night she slept, and this came.

### 12

*There was a peach tree, and it was not theirs.*

*It stood on the far side of a wire fence, in a yard where a dog lived. Every spring it put its flowers over the wire as if the wire were not there.*

*Her grandmother said a tree does not know about fences.*

*In summer the fruit came down on their side. It was small and hard, more stone than peach. Her grandmother boiled it with sugar she had been keeping for something else, and put it in a jar that had been for something else before it was for peaches.*

*She does not remember her grandmother's face. She remembers the hands, and the jar, and the dog, which never once barked at her.*

*A man came home that year with money folded in a handkerchief, and a blanket for the old woman, and he ate the peaches out of the jar with a spoon, standing up, and said they were the best thing he had tasted since he went down. He said "down" the way other people say "away."*

*He went back on the bus in September. The tree was in flower.*

*It would have fruited that summer the same as any other. Nobody on their side of the wire would have picked it up.*

*She has never liked peaches from a shop.*

*She has never asked herself why.*

Ruth woke at four with her face wet and no idea whose grandmother that had been. She had never had one that she knew of. She had never lived beside a wire fence. She lay and listened to the trucks going by on the main road, one every minute or so, low and heavy, taking coal somewhere in the dark.

There was no one to tell, so she got up and wrote it in her notebook, in the back, under a heading she made up on the spot: *Things I can't place.*

Then she turned to the front, where the facts were, and wrote: *Gun from desk. Who knew the drawer.*

### 13

On Wednesday she asked the car guard.

Every parking lot in South Africa has a man in it who will watch your car for a coin. He is not employed by anybody. He has a yellow bib that he bought himself. He stands in the sun for ten hours and sees everything that happens within a hundred metres, and no policeman has ever once asked him what it was.

The one outside Atlas House was called Petros. He was from Mozambique, and he had been guarding that lot for six years. He had opinions about every vehicle that used it.

"That one," he said, of Daniel Brandt's silver double cab, which was still there, "he never locked it. I told him. I said, *Chefe*, they have a machine now, they press it and your car doesn't lock, you walk away, they open it like a fridge. He said, Petros, there's nothing in it worth stealing except the truth, and nobody wants that." Petros shook his head. "He talked like that. On the radio also."

"You were here on Monday."

"Every day."

"Who ran?"

Petros considered this as if it were the first sensible question he had been asked in six years.

"Nobody," he said.

"Somebody shot a man and took his bag."

"Yes. But nobody ran. When they rob, they run, there's a car, the car goes." He made a noise with his mouth, tyres. "Monday, I'm there by the Spar side, I hear it, one time, *pah*. I come round. Mr Daniel is down. And nobody is running. There is no car going. There is only the door of the stairs, the side one, and it is closing by itself, slowly, like so."

He showed her with his hand. A door on a spring, easing shut.

"And then?"

"Then nothing, for a long time. Then Mr Sam comes out the front and he starts to shout for an ambulance."

"How long is a long time?"

Petros looked at the sky, where he kept his clock.

"Twenty minutes," he said. "I was thinking, why is nobody coming? In that building there is the guard, there is Mr Sam upstairs, there is the music children. Why is nobody coming to this man?"

She gave him fifty rand. It was Hendrik Brandt's fifty rand. Petros folded it into a very small square and put it in his sock.

### 14

Sam Nel was the technician. Every radio station has one. He is the man who knows which cable goes where, and he is never the one who talks.

He was thirty-one, pale and narrow, with a ginger beard he had grown in order to look older and which made him look like a boy in a beard. She found him on the fifth floor coiling cables that did not need coiling. His eyes were red. He had clearly been crying, and he had also clearly not been sleeping, and it was hard to say which had done more damage.

"I was in the back," he said. "With the headphones on. I was editing. I didn't hear anything. I came down for a smoke and he was just." He stopped. He started again. "I phoned straight away."

"From your phone."

"Ja."

"What time did you come down?"

"I don't know. Six? After six."

"He was shot at ten past."

"Then ten past. Quarter past. I don't know, I didn't look, I'm sorry, I'm not, I've never." He sat down on the couch, on the sleeping bag. "He was my friend. He was the only person in this town who ever said *well done* to me. Do you know that? For anything."

She believed him. That was the difficulty. Everything he said was true, and he said it like a man telling the truth, because he was. He had loved Daniel Brandt. He was sick with grief. He had also shot him. People think those things cannot both be in one body. They have not met many people.

"Who knew about the gun in the desk?" she asked.

Sam Nel went the colour of paper.

"What gun," he said.

That was the only lie he told her, and he told it so badly that she felt sorry for him.

### 15

**RADIO SKOORSTEEN. ARCHIVE. A SATURDAY IN AUGUST, 23:15.**

They're coming. Can you feel it? There's a pressure change. The cats know.

In two weeks they switch off the Beast for its annual scrape, and twenty thousand men are going to descend on this town like the Assyrian upon the fold, except that the Assyrian did not need a bed, a plate of pap and somewhere to charge his phone. If I wanted something done here, something I didn't want to do myself, that's when I'd have it done. I'm just saying. I'm a nervous man. I had two brandies with my supper and I'm seeing boilermakers in the curtains.

Here's Miriam Makeba. Lock your doors. Actually don't bother, they've got a machine for that now.

### 16

Grace Tau had a price list. It was not written down, but it was as fixed as the one at the Spar.

A rumour about someone's marriage was twenty rand. A rumour about someone's money was fifty. For a hundred she would tell you who was sleeping in which of her rooms, which firm they worked for and when they were paid. Above that it was by negotiation, and the price went up every day, because information is like fish.

On Wednesday evening Ruth put two hundred rand of Hendrik Brandt's money on the kitchen table between the sugar bowl and the biscuit tin with the bank cards in it.

"Sam Nel," she said.

Grace looked at the money and then at the stove, where the pap was.

"He owes me," she said. "Not him. The brother. Dawid. Dawid was at the mine, Brandtskraal, with the old man. Underground. They put him off three years ago with the others, that whole business, and he's got four children and a wife who doesn't work. So he comes to me." She shrugged. "They all came to me. Sixty men. What must they do, eat grass? But Dawid's got nothing, no house, no car, nothing I can hold. So the little brother signs. Sam. Sam's got a job. Sam stands for him."

"How much?"

"It was twelve thousand. It's forty-one now." She said this with no particular feeling, as you might read a thermometer. "That's what it does. I don't make it do that. That's just what money does when you leave it."

"And Sam couldn't pay."

"Sam could pay a little. Then Sam could pay nothing. Then Sam started paying another way."

She picked up the two hundred rand, folded it and put it down the front of her dress.

"He told me things," she said. "About the Brandt boy. What he was doing up there. Who he was phoning. What he had." She turned the pap. "I knocked a bit off, each time. Fair is fair."

"What did Daniel have?"

Grace Tau smiled at her for the first time. It was a warm, wide, motherly smile, and it made the room colder.

"That's a different price," she said. "That's not a two-hundred question, my girl. Half this town wants to know that. You come back when the old man's given you a bit more."

### 17

At seven that evening Ruth sat in her car at the edge of the lake.

There is a lake in the middle of Secunda. It is not a real one. The Swiss gentleman dammed a spruit to make it, because he thought a town should have water in it to look at, and for fifty years it has done its best. There are reeds, and a path, and coots. On the far bank there is a waterfront with a pizza place. On a still evening the plant lies upside down in it, towers and flames and all, and shivers when a duck goes by.

She had the radio on. She had found the frequency by turning the dial slowly until a dead man started shouting at her.

It was his last programme. He had recorded it on Sunday, and the computer in the corner, which did not know any better, put it out on Wednesday at seven as he had told it to. It went out once. She was one of perhaps a dozen people who heard it. Three of the others were the men who most wanted him to stop talking, and they sat in three separate vehicles in three separate parts of town and listened to every word.

### 18

**RADIO SKOORSTEEN. THE LAST ONE.**

Right. Deep breath. No brandy tonight, I want you to note that for the record. I am sober as a magistrate and twice as frightened.

I told you I knew something about a coal mine. I've been dancing round it for a month like a man round a snake. Tonight I'm going to pick it up.

When you sell coal, my darlings, you don't just tip it in a truck and wave. No. There is a *ceremony*. A little man takes a little scoop from the load, and he divides it in three, like Gaul. One part goes to the fellow buying. One part the mine keeps. And the third part goes in a tin, and the tin is sealed, and it sits on a shelf in the dark for ninety days in case anybody ever calls anybody a liar. It's called the umpire's sample. Isn't that lovely? Somewhere on a shelf, for every load of coal that ever left this district, there's a small sealed tin of the truth, waiting for a fight.

Now. Suppose, hypothetically, a mine not a hundred miles from this microphone has been sending out certificates for three years that say *this is coal*. Good coal! Burns like a Baptist! And suppose that what is actually in the trucks is, how shall I put this, *geology*. Rock, with a little coal on it for flavour. And suppose the certificates are signed, and stamped, and beautiful, and that the only thing in the entire world that says otherwise is a shelf of little tins.

And suppose the mine is about to be sold. On paper. For a great deal of money. To people who have read the certificates and not opened the tins.

You would want those tins, wouldn't you? If you were the seller you would want them in a furnace. If you were the buyer you would want them on your desk. If you were a certain type of person in between, you would want them in your safe, forever, so that the seller would have to be very nice to you for the rest of his life.

Well, nobody's got them. I have. Or rather I haven't, I'm not a fool, they are with a friend in eMbalenhle who keeps things safe, a man who has never opened a thing that wasn't his in sixty years, and God bless him for it, because it's a dying art.

And there's a book with them. I'll tell you about the book next week. The book is worse. The book goes back to the week sixty men downed tools three years ago, and it says *why*, in the lab's own handwriting, and when I read it I had to go and sit in my car.

Someone is coming up from Johannesburg on Monday to look at all this with a cold eye, because I don't trust mine any more. I'm too close. It's my father. Did I mention that? It's my father's mine. I'm the heir to the gravel.

So that's me. If I sound cheerful it's hysteria. Next week: the book. Same time. Tell the dog.

### 19

The programme ended. A song came on. Ruth turned the radio off and sat looking at the plant upside down in the water.

*A friend in eMbalenhle who keeps things safe.*

She thought of an old man at the back of a crowd with his hat in his hand, walking away as though he were carrying something.

Across the lake, in the car park of the pizza place, a white bakkie with a shield on its door started its engine and drove off without putting its lights on.

The wind came across the water. It smelled of reeds and mud and, faintly, of the sea. That was ridiculous. The sea was five hundred kilometres away and she had only ever seen it twice.

### 20

*Someone was telling a child about the sea.*

*He was a thin man with a blanket round his shoulders though it was not cold, and he sat on an upturned crate outside a long building with many doors, and he was laughing at her because she did not believe him.*

*It is like the veld, he said. You know the veld, when the grass is high and the wind goes over it? It is like that. It goes all the way to the end. But it is water.*

*And you can't walk on it, the child said.*

*You can't walk on it. That is the terrible thing. It looks as though you could. You stand there and every part of you says, go on, it's a field. My brother tried when he was small. My mother had to go in after him in her church dress.*

*He laughed until he coughed. He coughed for a long time.*

*Where I am from, he said, when a man dies they put him so he can hear it. That is all I want. I don't want a stone. I told them at the office, I wrote it on the form. If something happens, don't put me here. This ground is too quiet. Send me where I can hear it.*

*They didn't, said someone, a long time afterwards, in another voice. They couldn't. There was nothing to send.*

She woke on Thursday not knowing who the man had been, or the child, or what form he had written it on. It was not hers. She knew that much. Something in this town was using her to remember with, the way you might use a stranger's phone.

### 21

On Thursday she went to eMbalenhle to see the man who kept things safe.

It is a twenty-minute drive, and the road goes past the plant. This is by design. When the two towns were drawn, one for the people who would run the plant and one for the people who would work in it, the plant was put between them like a hand held up. From Secunda you see the towers. From eMbalenhle you see the towers. The wind blows mostly from the east, so one of the two towns gets the plume. You may guess which.

Joseph Sithole's house was in Extension 14. It was four rooms of plastered brick with a yard swept so clean that the broom marks showed. There was a peach tree here too. There are peach trees everywhere on the Highveld. People eat a peach and throw the stone over a wall, and forty years later somebody has a tree.

He gave her tea in an enamel mug and sat across the kitchen table from her with his glasses pushed up on his forehead. Behind him was a cabinet with photographs in it: children, a wedding, a football team of men in overalls squinting into the sun.

"I knew you'd come," he said. "I heard the radio."

"Everybody heard the radio."

"Not everybody. But the ones who shouldn't." He turned his mug a quarter turn on the plastic cloth. "He was a good boy. He talked too much. His father never talked at all, so maybe it was saved up."

"You worked for his father."

"Thirty-one years. Brandtskraal. I was the steward on Number Two section. And before that I was at the gold, at Kinross, as a boy."

He said the name as some people say the name of a hospital.

He got up and took a tin down from the top of the cabinet. It had once held shortbread. He opened it on the table between them. It was full of newspaper, folded small, and under the newspaper a few other things: a brass disc with a number stamped on it and a hole to hang it by, a union card, a child's tooth in a matchbox.

"September 1986," he said. "I was eighteen. I'd been underground four months. There was a fire. Not a big fire. A man was welding and it caught a gas bottle, and the tunnel was painted with a kind of foam, for the water, and the foam burned." He unfolded one of the cuttings with two fingers. It was the same headline Daniel had pinned to his wall. "It wasn't the fire that did it. It was the smoke off that foam. It went along the tunnel faster than a man can run. They found them sitting. Standing, some of them, holding their tools. A hundred and seventy-seven. They hadn't even put anything down."

The kitchen was quiet. Outside, a child was bouncing a ball against a wall.

"I was on the other level," Joseph said. "That's all. That's the only reason I'm an old man."

He picked up the brass disc.

"Lamp tag. You hang it on the board when you go down and you take it off when you come up, so they know. At the end of that shift there was a board with a hundred and seventy-seven tags still on it. This one was my friend's. He was from Lesotho, from up in the mountains. He told me once it snowed on the horses there. I thought he was joking." He put it down. "The company had a service. With a minister and chairs. They didn't ask us. They didn't ask the union. So we said no. We stopped work, the whole industry, one day, the first of October, and we had our own. In the hostels. In the veld. Everybody singing."

He folded the cutting up again along its old lines. It had been folded so many times that it was coming apart there.

"Nobody here remembers that," he said. "The young ones. You say Kinross, they think you mean the town, where the Engen is. It's in this tin. It's in a stone at Evander with the grass coming up over it. That's where they are now. In tins."

He put the lid back on.

"And in people's sleep," he said, looking at her quite directly. "Sometimes. If they're the kind."

She had not said a word about her dreams. She was sure of that.

"I came about Daniel," she said.

"Yes," said Joseph Sithole. "I know what you came about."

He did not offer her the crate. She did not ask for it. It was in the next room, under a blanket, close enough that she could have touched the wall it stood against. They drank their tea. He was waiting to see what kind she was. She was waiting too, though she could not have said for what. She had the patience of someone who has never been sure she was owed anything.

That was Thursday morning. If she had asked, he would have given it to her, and seven days later a woman named Anneline Petersen might not have been shot in her car. But she did not ask. Nobody ever tells you which cup of tea was the important one.

### 22

Thursday was also instalment day.

At five o'clock the men came to Grace Tau's back door. They came one at a time, down the side of the house past the green water tank, and they stood by the brazier with their caps in their hands as though it were a church. Each one paid, or explained. Those who paid got a tick in an exercise book. Those who explained got a look.

Ruth watched from the window of room four.

Most of them were in their fifties. They had the shoulders of men who had done one heavy thing for thirty years and then been told to stop. These were the sixty from Brandtskraal, or what was left of them. Three years ago they had stood in a tunnel and refused to do something they were told to do, and Hendrik Brandt had called it a strike, and a strike without the right paperwork is a thing you can be dismissed for. So he had dismissed them. There had been trouble at the gate. He had brought in a man with a khaki shirt and a helpful manner to deal with the trouble, and the man had dealt with it, and had somehow never left.

Their case was in the labour court in Johannesburg. It had been there for three years. It was expected to be heard quite soon.

Dawid Nel came last. He was a big, soft, ruined man with his brother's ginger colouring gone to grey. He had no money in his hand. Sam was with him.

She could not hear what was said. She saw Grace open the exercise book and turn it round and put her finger on a line. She saw Sam look at the line for a long time. She saw him say something, short. Grace nodded and drew a stroke through it with a ruler, and wrote a word in the margin, and the date.

Later, when the house was asleep and the brazier was a red eye in the yard, Ruth went down to the kitchen and found the exercise book in the drawer under the bank cards. She turned to the page.

*Nel D. / surety Nel S. R41 300.* A line ruled through it. In the margin, in Grace Tau's round, schoolgirl hand: *Settled in kind.*

And the date. It was Monday's date.

She looked at it for a while.

Grace Tau did her books in the morning. Everyone in the house knew that: you did not disturb her before ten, she was at her figures. So on Monday morning, with her tea, she had written off forty-one thousand rand of Sam Nel's debt in exchange for something he had told her. And at ten past six that evening Daniel Brandt had been shot through the chest by somebody he let stand close.

The debt had been cleared before he died. That was the thing. Whatever Sam had sold her, he had sold it that morning, and it had been worth forty-one thousand rand, and by afternoon Daniel had found out.

She put the book back exactly as it had been. She was good at that.

### 23

That same evening she climbed four floors of Atlas House, because she had heard music coming down the stairwell and wanted to know what kind of people made music in a building where a man had just been killed.

The treble clef on the green door had been drawn by a child. Behind the door was a hall with a parquet floor, lifting at the corners, and a row of windows that looked out over the parking lot toward the plant. There were twenty-odd teenagers in a half circle with instruments, and behind them on a set of wooden steps a dozen adults holding black folders. In front of them all stood a small bald man in shirtsleeves, with a pencil where a baton should have been.

His name was Mr February. He had taught music at a high school for thirty years, and when the school stopped teaching music he had simply carried on, here, on Tuesday and Thursday evenings, for anyone who would climb the stairs. The instruments were donated. Some had been donated by people who had not known they were donating them.

"From the top," he said. "And cellos, it's a lullaby. You are trying to put a baby to sleep. You are not trying to saw it in half."

They played.

It was a tune she knew. Everybody knows it. It is the tune somebody's grandmother sang, in one language or another, in every kitchen between here and the Limpopo: *hush, be still, your father will be home by morning.* Mr February had arranged it for strings and voices. It began with one cello by itself, very low, and that was a girl of about fourteen with her tongue between her teeth and her hair in two puffs, who never took her eyes off her own left hand.

Ruth stood inside the door. Nobody looked at her. She was used to that; it was how she made her living.

In the back row of the choir, among the basses, an old man with a grey moustache was singing with his eyes shut. It was Joseph Sithole. He had come in on the five o'clock taxi, as he did every Tuesday and Thursday. When they stopped he opened his eyes and saw her, and nodded, once, like a man acknowledging someone across a street.

"That's Captain Khoza's girl," whispered a woman beside her, a mother, waiting with a lunch box. "The cello. Thando. He never comes. He's always working." She sighed. "The concert's for Heritage Day. Two weeks. You must come. Nobody ever comes."

Ruth looked round the hall, because that was what she did in rooms. Two ways out. The double doors she had come through, onto the main stair. And at the far end a green door with a running man on it, which gave onto the iron fire stair down the side of the building. Six little practice rooms along one wall. A storeroom. A trestle table with an urn.

She did not know why she was noting it. It was only habit. A week later she could have drawn that floor in the dark, and it was as well that she could.

### 24

The first bus to Johannesburg left the taxi rank at six on Friday morning.

The rank was near the corner of two streets named for men who would not have liked each other. At half past five it was already awake. Women were lighting braziers under pots. Minibuses stood in rows with their doors open and their engines running, and men walked between them shouting the names of places, Bethal, Standerton, Ermelo, like auctioneers selling the same thing over and over. Along one wall a line of tents and cardboard had been put up by people who had nowhere else. Someone had hung washing between two of them.

It was cold. The Highveld is cold at that hour even in spring. The smoke from the braziers lay flat in the air at the height of a man's chest and did not rise, and it smelled of coal and porridge and paraffin.

Ruth stood in it with her hands in her blazer pockets and felt the world tilt slightly, as it had beside the peach tree.

*Not now,* she thought.

Sam Nel was sitting on the kerb beside the Johannesburg bus with a sports bag between his feet. He had shaved off the beard. He looked about nineteen.

He saw her and did not get up. He looked almost relieved, like a man in a waiting room whose name has finally been called.

She sat down on the kerb next to him. It seemed the decent thing.

"I've been to see Grace's book," she said.

"Ja."

"It's dated Monday."

"Ja." He rubbed his face with both hands. "She said if I told her what he had, properly, all of it, she'd wipe the whole thing. Dawid's whole thing. Four years he's been paying her and it only gets bigger, do you know that? He paid her eighteen thousand and he owes her forty-one. I don't even understand how. I did maths."

"So you told her about the crate."

"I told her what was in it. The tins, the book, that he'd got them out of the lab, that he was going to say it on the radio. I didn't know where he'd put them, he never told me that, he said it was better I didn't know. I thought, what does it matter? He's going to tell the whole town on Wednesday anyway. I thought, she'll sell it to somebody two days early and it won't change anything, and Dawid can sleep." He laughed, a small dry sound. "I was so *happy*. Monday morning. I bought a pie."

A minibus hooted. A woman went by with a baby on her back and a bucket on her head, and the baby looked at them over its mother's shoulder with enormous calm.

"And then?"

"And then at five o'clock he comes in and he knows. I don't know how he knows. Grace must have sold it already, somebody must have phoned him. And he's not even *angry*." Sam's voice went up and cracked. "That's the thing. If he'd shouted at me. He just stood there by the desk and he looked so tired, and he said, *Sam. Ag, Sam. Why didn't you just ask me for the money?*"

He was crying now, without any noise, the tears going down into his collar.

"And I couldn't. I couldn't stand it. That he was going to forgive me. That he'd already done it, I could see it on his face, he'd forgiven me before he'd finished the sentence, and I was going to have to walk around for the rest of my life being a person he'd forgiven. And the drawer was open. I'd been looking for a cable. It was just there."

"You followed him down."

"He said he was going out for air. I went down the side stairs. I came round the front and he was standing by his bakkie with his keys, and he turned round, and he smiled at me." He wiped his nose on his sleeve. "I took the phone and the bag afterwards. So it would look like. I don't know what I thought it would look like. I threw them in the dam. Then I went back up and I sat on the floor for a bit. Then I phoned."

"Twenty minutes."

"Was it? It felt like I was up there a year."

So that was that. That was who killed Daniel Brandt, and why. It had nothing to do with coal. It had to do with forty-one thousand rand, and a kind man's face, and a drawer that happened to be open. It was the size of most murders. Most murders would fit in a kitchen.

Ruth felt no triumph. She had not expected to. She took out her phone.

### 25

Captain Khoza came himself, in the car that started.

A young constable was driving it. She was perhaps twenty-six, with a round, serious face under her cap, and she parked across the front of the Johannesburg bus as though she had been waiting three years for the chance to do so. Her name was Lerato Nkuna. She got out, looked at Sam Nel on the kerb, looked at Ruth, and held the back door open for him like a chauffeur.

He got in without being asked. He left his bag on the pavement. Nkuna picked it up and put it in beside him.

Khoza stood with his hands in the pockets of his brown jacket and watched the bus pull out round them.

"He told you all that," he said. "Just now. Sitting there."

"Yes."

"With nobody writing it down."

"No."

He nodded slowly. He looked like a man who has been given a present he cannot afford to keep.

"You know what I've got now, Mma? I've got a boy in my car who'll say it again at the station, today, because he wants to. And by Monday he'll have a lawyer from Legal Aid who'll say he was confused. And the gun is in the dam, and I haven't got divers. I'd have to ask Provincial for divers. They'll send them in November." He sighed. "On Monday I had a robbery. A robbery was *finished.* Now I've got a murder with a confession and no weapon, and a rifle cartridge under a flower box that somebody wants me to care about very much."

"I'm sorry."

"No, no." He waved a hand. "Don't be sorry. It's the truth. I joined for that, a long time ago. I just don't get it very often, so I don't know where to put it."

He looked at her properly for the first time since Monday night.

"You'll tell the old man."

"He hired me to."

"Then he'll pay you and you'll go." He said it without bitterness. "Everybody goes. It's that kind of town. Thank you for my murderer."

He got into the front seat. Nkuna reversed, very carefully, round a woman selling vetkoek, and they went off up the street with Sam Nel's pale face in the back window, looking at nothing.

### 26

That night was payday.

Twenty thousand contractors had money in their pockets for the first time in two weeks, and by nine o'clock the cash machines along the main street had queues of forty men each. Grace Tau walked along the queues in her good coat. She did not say anything. She only walked, and men who saw her remembered things, and came out of the queue to speak to her.

The taverns filled. In the car parks, boot lids went up and music came out. In eMbalenhle the smoke of a thousand suppers went up and joined the plume. Somewhere a man won eleven hundred rand at dice and somewhere else a man lost it. It was the same eleven hundred rand.

Shortly after one in the morning, outside a tavern in Extension 18, a scaffolder from Empangeni had a disagreement with a pipe fitter from Tzaneen about a woman neither of them had spoken to. It lasted about as long as it takes to read this sentence. The pipe fitter had a knife. The scaffolder sat down against a wall with his hand pressed to his side and a look of mild surprise. He was twenty-four. He had a daughter he had seen twice.

The police were called at ten past one. The station had no vehicle to send. One was in the workshop, and the other was parked outside Captain Khoza's house, because he was the only one trusted to bring it back.

Nobody was sent.

In the morning a van came from Evander and took the scaffolder to Mr Zwane, who added him to the count on his fingers.

### 27

**RADIO SKOORSTEEN. ARCHIVE. A SUNDAY IN JULY, 02:05.**

I had lunch with my father today. I want to tell you about it, because I've had three brandies and you're the only people I know who can't interrupt.

He lives out on the farm. *The* farm. Brandtskraal. My great-grandfather grew mealies on it and my grandfather grew mealies on it and my father looked at it one day in 1979 and thought, you know what's under these mealies? And he was right! God, he was right. He dug a hole in my grandfather's top field and money came out of it for forty years. I was raised on that hole. That hole paid for my teeth.

He's seventy-eight now and he's on a machine. It breathes for him. It's the lungs, obviously it's the lungs, forty years of standing next to his own stockpile. There's a justice in it if you like that sort of thing. I don't. Maybe he did. That's the horror. Maybe every single thing wrong with this place was done by somebody for somebody else's good, with a clean conscience, on a Tuesday.

I'm going to bed. I've upset myself. Here's some gospel.

### 28

They buried Daniel Brandt on Saturday at ten, from the big Dutch Reformed church with the roof like a ski slope, and all four of them came.

Ruth sat at the back, where she could see.

Hendrik Brandt was wheeled in by a nurse, with the machine on a hook under his chair. He sat in the aisle at the front and looked straight ahead at the box his son was in. He did not cry. Nobody had expected him to. But the hand on the blanket shook the whole hour, and this time he did not look at it.

Marius Steyn sat two rows behind him in a suit, with his wife. He sang the hymns in a strong, pleasant baritone, and found the place for the old lady beside him.

Abel Mokoena came late and stood by the door. He was a square, heavy man in his fifties in a good leather jacket, with a gold watch and the eyes of someone who has had to count his change his whole life. He owned eleven trucks. He was the chairman of a thing called the Forum, which existed so that when a contract was given out at a mine in that district, some of it would be given to people who lived there. This was a reasonable idea. It was enforced by men who stood at gates. Half the town thought he had ordered the man in the box killed, and he knew it, and he had come anyway and stood where everyone could look at him. That took a kind of courage.

Grace Tau sat with the church ladies. She had brought a tray of scones.

Captain Khoza stood at the back near Ruth, in his brown jacket. He had Sam Nel in a cell and he had not told anyone but the old man. He was waiting to see what the town would do when it found out that its hit was a boy with a debt.

Joseph Sithole was not there. It was not his church. He was at home with his tin.

The dominee spoke about a light that had gone out. Ruth watched the backs of four heads and thought about a shelf of small sealed tins.

Afterwards, in the hall, there was tea from an urn and Grace Tau's scones, and they came to her one at a time. It was like a dance. None of them saw the others do it.

Steyn came first, with a cup in each hand, and gave her one. "A terrible thing," he said. "And the Captain tells Oom Hendrik it was the technician. Over money! You'd never think." He blew on his tea. Mokoena came second, and did not bother with tea. "You're the one who found the boy," he said. "So you know it wasn't my drivers. Good. Tell them. Nobody listens to me." He looked at her from under his eyebrows. "He had some tins. I want them. Not to burn. I've been docked nine hundred thousand rand this year for delivering stones. *My* drivers, they say, swapping the load on the road. I have never swapped a load in my life. Those tins say what went in the truck at the mine. You understand what that is to me? That's my name."

Grace Tau came third, with the scones. "You didn't come back with more money," she said, "so I'll tell you this one free. Daniel borrowed from me too. For his licence, for the radio. He put up a box of something as security. I never saw it. But it's mine, by rights, until the estate pays." She held out the tray. "Have one. They're yesterday's."

And last, when the hall was nearly empty and the nurse was fetching the car, Hendrik Brandt.

He had her brought to him. He sat in his chair by the door with the autumn sun on one side of his face.

"Nel," he said. "The technician."

"Yes."

"Over a loan."

"His brother's. His brother was one of your sixty."

Something went across his face then that she could not read. It was there and gone, like a bird across a window. She thought afterwards that it might have been arithmetic. A man doing a sum, and not liking the answer, and doing it again.

"I see," he said.

He took an envelope from under the blanket. It was thick.

"That is what he owed you, and the same again, as I said. You've done what I asked. It took you five days. I had expected to be disappointed." The machine sighed. "My son had a box. Lab samples. They are of no value to anyone, they are old rejects that should have been thrown away, but they belong to the company and I'd like them back before Friday. If you were to come across them, there would be another envelope."

"What's on Friday?"

"I'm selling my mine, Miss Mahlangu. To some gentlemen from Johannesburg. I'm an old man and I'm tired and I have nobody now to leave it to." He looked at her with his pale hard eyes. "Go home. You've been very efficient. Go home."

The nurse came and wheeled him out into the light.

So that made four people who wanted the crate, and each had told her it was a different thing. Company property. A man's good name. Security on a loan. Old rejects of no value to anyone. She stood in the church hall holding an envelope of money and a cold cup of tea. She understood that she had been hired for one job and dropped into the middle of another, and that the first job had been the easy one.

*They stood at the fence for days, and then for more days.*

*The company drilled holes from the top, to find them, to send down air and a microphone. There were four hundred and thirty-five of them down there. On the last day the drill broke through into the place where they should have been, and they lowered the microphone, and everybody at the fence stopped breathing so as to hear.*

*There was water. That was all. You could hear it dripping.*

*They are still there. Nobody was ever brought up. They put a stone on top.*

*The shirts stayed on the line all that time. When she came home they were stiff as boards with the dust, and she took them down, and folded them, and put them in the drawer.*

Ruth sat up in the dark with her heart going.

It was twenty to five. Out in the yard, an engine started. She went to the window and moved the curtain an inch.

At five on Thursday morning the tyres went up.

That was a long day. It was the longest of the fourteen.

Forty people had been on that floor. Forty walked down. Not one had so much as turned an ankle. It should be said. Not much went right in that fortnight.

Joseph Sithole came out of the instrument store. He was carrying something wrapped in a grey blanket, with some difficulty, because it was heavy. He set it down on the conductor's stand in front of her and folded the blanket back.

It was a wooden crate with rope handles. In it were forty tins, each the size of a jam tin, each sealed round the lid with red wax. On every seal were two initials, pressed in with a thumbnail. On top lay a hardcover book with a marbled spine.

"You said when she'd spoken for it," Ruth said.

"She can't now." He laid his hand flat on the book. "So it'll have to be you."

She looked at the tins. They were the truth, on a shelf, waiting for a fight. But they could not talk any more. The woman whose thumbnail was in the wax was under a sheet in Evander. The only man who could have said who paid for that lay on the other side of the door. What Ruth had in front of her was forty jam tins of dirt, and a book in two colours of ink.

She had been five days late for Daniel Brandt, an hour late for Anneline Petersen, and one second late for a truck driver called Bheki. She was beginning to see that it was not bad luck. It was her nature. She arrived, and looked carefully, and understood. It was always afterwards.

In the corner of the hall the red lamp of a speaker was lit. The radio station was only one floor up; it still fed every corridor in the building. All through that hour, under the cello, under everything, a dead man had been talking quietly to himself on the fifth floor about coal.

## PART FOUR: GETTING USED TO IT

### 53

She drove out to the farm before it was light. The tyres were only rings of grey ash on the tar by then, with wire in them.

Nobody stopped her at the gate. The house had one lamp burning, as before. The nurse let her in without surprise and went back to bed.

Hendrik Brandt was in his chair. He had not been to bed. He was dressed for the day, in a jacket and tie, with the blanket over his knees, and he looked like something that has been left out all night.

Steyn had telephoned him at eleven. She could tell. He knew how many were dead and in what order.

She had brought one tin. She put it on the tea tray, between the milk jug and the sugar. It sat there with its red wax seal and the two small initials, among the good china.

He looked at it for a long while.

"What do you want?" he said.

She found that she did not want anything. She had thought, driving out, that she would. She had thought there would be something to say to him. She had turned sentences over in the car. But she was not a person who said things. She was a person who looked.

So she looked at him. That was all she did. She sat in the chair opposite, in the lamplight, with her hands in her lap, and looked at an old man in a tie.

It was a good deal harder for him than shouting would have been.

"Four hundred and twelve," he said at last. "That's how many are on the payroll. I know every one. I knew their fathers." The machine sighed. "You think I did it for the money. I've got no use for money. What would I buy? I did it so that on the first of next month four hundred and twelve people would have something in the bank. That's all I've ever done. Forty years. I kept the plates full."

She said nothing.

"I never told him to do it. Marius. I want that understood."

"What did you tell him?"

His mouth worked.

"I said he must do what he thought best."

"Yes," Ruth said.

The lamp hummed. Outside, down the hill, the day shift was coming on, a string of headlights turning in at the gate.

"The Nel boy," Hendrik Brandt said. He was not looking at her now. He was looking at the tin. "He told you my son smiled at him."

"He said so."

"At the end."

"Yes."

"He would have." The old man's face did something then. It did not weep. He was not able to; that part of him had been closed for too long, like a room in a big house. But the hand on the blanket went up and covered his eyes and stayed there. "He'd have forgiven him, the fool. He'd have forgiven me. He'd have stood there with that bloody microphone and told eleven people what I'd done, and then he'd have come to lunch on Sunday."

The machine went on breathing for him. It did not know how to stop.

After a time he took the hand away.

"Take your tin," he said. "Do what you like with it. I'll be at the office at ten. I've never been late for a meeting in my life."

She took it. At the door she turned round, because she felt she owed him one true thing, and she had only one.

"He kept cuttings," she said. "On his wall, at the station. Kinross. Middelbult. Coalbrook. He'd gone over the numbers in pen."

Hendrik Brandt closed his eyes.

"I know," he said. "I gave them to him. The oldest one was my father's."

### 54

At ten o'clock on Friday the buyers sat down at the long table in the mine office, with a fountain pen each.

The contract was two hundred pages. It lay in the middle of the table in a blue folder, with yellow tags sticking out where people were to sign. The laboratory report from Johannesburg lay beside it. It had come by email the night before, and it was a beautiful report. The man who was buying had read it in his hotel room with a whisky and felt like a boy.

His boots were no longer the colour of butter. He had been walking round a coal mine in them for four days. He was rather proud of that.

Hendrik Brandt sat at the head of the table in his jacket and tie. Marius Steyn stood by the window with his hat in his hands, a helpful presence, in case anybody needed anything.

Nobody had told the men from Johannesburg what had happened in town the night before. Why would they? It was a drugs matter. It had nothing to do with coal.

The door opened.

Ruth came in first. Behind her came Joseph Sithole in his church jacket, and behind him his nephew Vusi, carrying the crate by its rope handles as easily as a tray of bread. Behind them, in the passage, thirty men in their fifties stood with their caps in their hands and did not come in.

Vusi set the crate on the table, on top of the contract.

"I'm sorry," said one of the lawyers. "Who are—"

"These are the retained samples for the last ninety days," Ruth said. "One for every load that left this mine. They were sealed at the time by the woman who ran the laboratory. That's her mark in the wax. She was shot dead yesterday morning at her gate." She laid the book beside the crate. "This is her day book. The real figures are in blue. The ones on your certificates are in black. The samples your man took on Monday were changed that night. I have photographs. They aren't very good."

It was the most she had said at one time since she came to Secunda. She had practised it in the car.

Nobody spoke.

The sunburnt young geologist, whom nobody had introduced, got up. She came round the table and took a tin out of the crate, and turned it over, and looked at the seal for some time. Then she took a penknife out of her pocket and ran it round the wax.

She tipped the tin out onto the blue folder.

It was grey. That was all. It was crushed fine, as sand is, and it was the grey of a pavement. Here and there in it was a black grain, like pepper in salt.

She rubbed some between her finger and thumb. She smelled it. She looked at the man who was buying.

"That's parting," she said. "That's the rock between the seams. There's maybe one part in five of coal in that." She opened another tin, and another. They were all the same. "You couldn't burn this in a brazier."

The man who was buying looked at the grey heaps on his contract. He looked down the table at Hendrik Brandt, who met his eye, and did not look away, and did not say a word.

Then he looked at his boots.

He screwed the cap back on his pen. He did it slowly and carefully. He stood up, and buttoned his jacket, and went out past the thirty men in the passage without seeing them. His lawyers went after him, already on their phones.

The two dark cars left at twenty past ten.

That was the end of Brandtskraal Colliery. It did not take long. It had been a farm, and then a hole, and now it was a hole with nobody in it. Four hundred and twelve people were given notice the following Friday. There was no severance. There was no one to pay it.

Ruth had known that when she carried the crate in. She had done the sum in the car, as everyone else in this story did a sum. Eight months of wages on a lie, or nothing on the truth. She had looked at it squarely, and she had chosen the truth. She would never know whether that was because it was right, or only because it was the thing she had been hired, eleven days before, to bring.

Marius Steyn put his hat on and left by the side door. Nobody stopped him. There was nothing to stop him for.

Joseph Sithole picked up the book with the marbled spine. He held it in both hands for a moment, as you hold a hymnal.

"This one's ours," he said. "For the lawyers. It says why we stopped."

"It's yours," Ruth said.

He nodded. At the door he turned.

"You did right, my daughter," he said. "It won't feel like it."

It did not.

### 55

That night they started the plant again.

It is a thing to see. For a month the towers had been cold and the flames small and blue. On Friday evening, one unit after another, the plant was lit, and it had to clear its throat. Everything in the pipes that was not wanted went up the flare stacks and burned. The flames stood sixty metres high, the colour of a peeled orange, roaring so that you could hear it in Evander. The whole sky over the town went the red of the inside of an eyelid. You could have read a newspaper on your lawn at midnight.

People did come out onto their lawns. They brought chairs. Children ran about with long shadows. It happened every year, and every year the town came out to look at it, as other towns turn out for fireworks.

Ruth stood in Grace Tau's yard among the bakkies and watched it. The neat man's was gone; the police had taken it. The plate had been stolen in Bloemfontein in May.

The contractors would be leaving in a week. The shutdown was over. Twenty thousand strangers would go back to wherever they came from, and the town would be forty thousand people again, who knew one another, and had got used to it.

Grace came out and stood beside her, wiping her hands on her apron.

"There's water," she said. "It came on at six. It's brown, but it's water. You can wash your hair."

She slept that night with the window wide open and the sky burning, and the wind blew straight in off the plant, and it brought her one more.

The fifteenth of September. A Monday. She counted on her fingers in the dark. Tuesday would have been the sixteenth.

She understood then that she would never find out why these came to her. Nobody would step out of a doorway on the last morning and say her father's name. There was no father. There was no doorway. A place had needed something to remember with, and she had been lying there with her window open.

She got up and washed her hair. The water was brown, as promised. It smelled of iron, like blood, or a key.

The smell came in. She hardly noticed it. That frightened her more than anything else that had happened.

Then the town fell behind and the veld opened. It was brown and enormous, with the black strips of the firebreaks along the fences and the conveyor walking across it on its steel legs. The wind came over all that grass and in at the window. It smelled of dust, and burnt stubble, and far off, on the edge of it, something sweet. It was peach blossom. There was a farmhouse somewhere, with a tree.

For a moment she had it.

It was right there. It was a yard, and a wire fence, and a woman's hands, and a word in a language she had once known how to speak. It was the name of a place. It was on her tongue. She opened her mouth to say it.

A truck went by the other way, loaded, and the car rocked, and the window filled with the roar of it.

When it was quiet again there was only the road.

## WHAT BECAME OF THEM

**Samuel Nel** pleaded guilty to the murder of Daniel Brandt and was sentenced in the High Court sitting at Delmas to fifteen years. The pistol was recovered from the lake by police divers in November. His brother's debt to Grace Tau was never reinstated. She had written *settled*, and she was a woman of her word.

**The man in room six** pleaded guilty to the murder of Anneline Petersen and was sentenced to eighteen years. In terms of his agreement with the State he named the person who had engaged him. It was a truck driver, deceased. Asked by the judge who had engaged the truck driver, he said that he did not know, and the judge said that she believed him.

**Bhekisisa Zungu**, 29, was buried from his mother's house in eMbalenhle. The sports bag contained sixty thousand rand. It was entered as an exhibit and has not been seen since.

**Kobus Venter** and three colleagues gave statements. No charges were brought. They had responded to a report of shots fired at premises they were contracted to protect.

**Marius Steyn** was not charged with anything. His firm's contract at Atlas House was renewed in January. In December he lent the police four vehicles for the festive season roadblocks, as he did every year.

**Hendrik Brandt** died in his chair the following February, of his lungs. The machine was still running when the nurse found him. He left the farm to nobody. It is in the hands of the Master of the High Court, who is considering the matter.

**Brandtskraal Colliery** closed on the ninth of October. Four hundred and twelve people were retrenched. The shaft was sealed with a concrete plug. By the following winter there were men going down it at night through a hole in the fence, with head torches and buckets, to dig what was left.

**The sixty** won their case. The Labour Court found, on the evidence of a laboratory day book kept in two colours of ink, that they had been dismissed for refusing an unlawful instruction, and ordered their reinstatement with three years' back pay. The judgment was handed down nineteen months after the mine closed. The company no longer existed. Joseph Sithole had the judgment framed. It is on the wall above his cabinet, next to the football team.

**Captain Sizwe Khoza** was commended. The Petersen and Atlas House dockets were transferred to a provincial unit in Mbombela, where they remain open. He attended the Heritage Day concert and sat in the front row. He has attended every one since.

**The ballistics report** came back in July. The pistol that killed Anneline Petersen had been used once before, in a shooting at the gate of Brandtskraal Colliery three years earlier, in which a shaft steward was wounded in the leg. That docket had been in Captain Khoza's tall pile the whole time. It is still there. It has a note stapled to it now.

**Constable Lerato Nkuna** is still driving the car.

**Abel Mokoena** was repaid nine hundred thousand rand in haulage penalties by three former customers of the colliery, with interest. He tells people he is owed a favour by a detective in Johannesburg. He has not yet said what it is.

**Grace Tau** still sells eggs on a Friday.

**Thando Khoza** is studying music at the university in Pretoria. She plays the cello. When people ask her about that night, she says that she does not remember being frightened. She remembers that she was trying not to rush.

**Radio Skoorsteen** went on broadcasting for five weeks after its owner was buried, until someone remembered the generator and switched it off. The licence application is still pending.

The scaffolder from Empangeni, the woman from Kinross, the man with the television and the woman who waited for the ambulance are in the tall pile.

Nobody was sent.

**Ruth Mahlangu** went back to Johannesburg. She still dreams, though less often. She has never been able to find the yard with the peach tree, and she has stopped looking. She keeps a notebook by the bed. In the back of it, under the heading *Things I can't place*, there are now eleven entries. The last one is a single line, in her careful hand, with nothing after it:

*It snows on the horses there.*
