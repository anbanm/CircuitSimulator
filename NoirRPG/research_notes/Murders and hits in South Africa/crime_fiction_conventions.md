## 1. Novels: main authors, detectives, settings, and the critical debate about the post-apartheid genre

### Takeaway
The post-1994 "krimi" boom is dominated by white, Cape-based authors writing police or ex-police protagonists (Meyer, Orford, Nicol, Smith), with a smaller set of Black-authored works (Makholwa, Mzobe, Golakai, Mhlongo's *Joburg Noir*) that critics single out for writing from inside the township or the new middle class instead of looking down from the state. The academic argument is whether this fiction is "the new political novel" (Gilfillan, Naidu and le Roux, de Kock in part) or a genre machine that exaggerates state criminality for effect (de Kock's own caveat).

**The critical debate**

- Leon de Kock, *Losing the Plot: Crime, Reality and Fiction in Postapartheid Writing* (Wits University Press, 2016): post-apartheid writing responds to "plot loss" in two ways, underplotting in high literary fiction and overplotting in genre fiction; crime fiction and literary non-fiction are treated as the leading post-apartheid forms because of their strong relation to "the real". He reads Meyer's *Heart of the Hunter* as "a form of social hermeneutics", draws on Jean and John Comaroff on crime in late modernity, and likens contemporary crime writing to frontier adventure testing whether social order can be restored. [EXTRACT] — [LitNet review](https://www.litnet.co.za/losing-plot-crime-reality-fiction-postapartheid-writing-leon-de-kock-book-review/); [Tydskrif vir Letterkunde review](https://letterkunde.africa/article/view/2980); [Postcolonial Text review](https://www.postcolonial.org/index.php/pct/article/viewFile/2343/2171); [Mail & Guardian, 2015](https://mg.co.za/article/2015-04-23-post-liberation-writing/)
- De Kock in his own words (2012): Lynda Gilfillan suggested crime fiction may have become "the new 'political novel'"; de Kock agrees that "crime thrillers are about the most operative and readable kinds [of political fiction] we have going right now", because the older political novel had become "relentlessly tedious social realism" with "mythologisation of struggle-hero types". [FULL] — [Daily Maverick / SLiP, 23 Jan 2012](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2012-01-23-sa-crime-thrillers-and-the-genre-snob-debate/)
- De Kock's caveat in the same essay: Smith and Nicol tie every crime to ministerial corruption, "the tentacles spreading downwards like so many evil fingers", and the real world contradicts that totality (two police commissioners were caught and punished). He asks whether "genre ... overpowers responsibly complex historical adequation". [FULL] — [same article](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2012-01-23-sa-crime-thrillers-and-the-genre-snob-debate/)
- De Kock on Mzobe as the alternative: "Mzobe writes from the point of view of the township, and he does not make large assumptions about state corruption ... What happens to characters in and of the townships are crystallisations of township conditions." He calls *Young Blood* a crime novel, an anti-Bildungsroman and an implicitly political novel at once, and says the "krimi" category "has perhaps now outlived its purpose". [FULL] — [same article](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2012-01-23-sa-crime-thrillers-and-the-genre-snob-debate/)
- The "genre snob" debate ran on SLiPnet and Nicol's Crime Beat blog and at the 2012 Franschhoek Literary Festival; the question was whether crime fiction "provides a grammar with which to explore and critique the current political moment". [EXTRACT] — [LitNet](https://www.litnet.co.za/crime-fiction-in-south-africa-the-history-the-hype-and-the-genre-snob-debate/)
- Sam Naidu and Elizabeth le Roux, "South African crime fiction: sleuthing the State post-1994" (*African Identities*): argues for the genre as a "new political novel" on the basis of its capacity for socio-political analysis. [EXTRACT] — [University of Pretoria repository](https://repository.up.ac.za/items/c7896a08-fef9-4dd3-af46-086f01cbeaa7)
- Naidu, "Crime Fiction, South Africa: A Critical Introduction" (*Current Writing* 25:2, 2013): distinguishes two sub-genres, the crime thriller and the literary detective novel, and notes that Nicol's Crime Beat blog was the main forum for debate. [EXTRACT] — [CORE PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/145031139.pdf)
- Naidu and le Roux, *A Survey of South African Crime Fiction* (UKZN Press, 2017): first comprehensive survey, with a bibliography from the 1890s. A Project MUSE review says the book holds that the genre is "ideologically ambiguous" because (white) writers aim to expose the "rottenness" of the post-apartheid state, and notes the authors attend more to patriarchy than to racism. [EXTRACT] — [Project MUSE review](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/742413/pdf)
- Christopher Warnes, "Writing Crime in the New South Africa: Negotiating Threat in the Novels of Deon Meyer and Margie Orford" (*JSAS* 38:4, 2012): reads the fiction as a way of negotiating threat; his line that the detective "returns to South African literature with a vengeance" is cited by le Roux against the claim that the genre is brand new. [EXTRACT] — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/cjssxx/v38y2012i4p981-991.html); [le Roux bibliography essay, CORE](https://core.ac.uk/download/80904919.pdf)
- Glenn Harper (LARB) holds that Orford and Meyer "exemplify the vitality and social conscience of South African crime fiction". [EXTRACT] — [LA Review of Books](https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/devils-merry-go-round-cape-town-noir/)
- Counter-view from inside the genre: Nicol in 2010 called the boom "an indicator that society itself is normalising" and described crime fiction as commercial, accessible writing that still "does tackle the big themes in a society". [FULL] — [Daily Maverick, 24 Nov 2010](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2010-11-24-bookex-sas-crime-writers-strut-their-stuff/)
- Orford's later repudiation of the form's premise: "I couldn't continue with the illusion that the police would help restore social balance. After the Marikana massacre, I thought that I can't go on pretending. The police were functioning as they were under apartheid, as a paramilitary force to keep corrupt people in power." She scrapped a sixth Clare Hart book because Hart "was reproducing the male gaze that I was trying to interrogate". [FULL] — [Daily Maverick, Joy Watson, 23 Nov 2022](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2022-11-23-margie-orford-focuses-our-gaze-on-secrets-obsession-and-intergenerational-trauma-and-produces-an-exquisite-work-of-art/)

### Inferences
- Two structural habits define the mainstream: (a) the investigator belongs to or orbits the state (cop, profiler, ex-cop, journalist), and (b) the crime is explained top-down by elite corruption. De Kock's contrast with Mzobe names the alternative: explain crime bottom-up from local conditions and write from inside the community.
- The strongest critique of the police-procedural frame comes from a leading practitioner (Orford), not only from academics. After Marikana, a story in which a detective "restores order" in a mining town carries a specific credibility problem the writers should address head-on.
- "First Black crime writer" claims for Makholwa (2007) conflict with the Drum-era record (section 7) and with Gomolemo Mokae's earlier work (see Gaps). The accurate statement is that Black-authored crime fiction was scarce in the 1994 to 2007 boom, not absent from the tradition.

## 2. Settings: what is saturated, what is rare, and anything set in coal or mining towns

### Takeaway
Cape Town and the Cape Flats, Johannesburg, Durban/KZN and Karoo or Northern Cape farm dorps carry almost all the fiction and prestige television. I found no crime novel, series or film set in Secunda, Witbank/Emalahleni, Middelburg or Ermelo; the nearest are a little-known coalfields fraud thriller by a mining lawyer (*Steal a Few Cents*), two thrillers set in isolated mining towns elsewhere, a diamond-mine telenovela and a Netflix family drama titled *Magenta Coal*. Mining communities appear mostly in documentary and stage work about Marikana.

### Inferences
- A detective story set in a Mpumalanga coal and petrochemical town has almost no direct precedent in fiction or on screen. The freshness is real, but so is the absence of a model: there is no established way of writing Highveld coal-town speech, class structure or landscape in the genre.
- Existing mining-town thrillers (*Memo from Turner*, *Salamander Cotton*, *The River*) lean on "town owned by one powerful figure". That is the nearest thing to a worn mining-town convention and it overlaps with the *Red Harvest* structure (section 7).
- Booysen's memoir detail (hostels, rock drillers as the poorest whites, labour type as a dividing line alongside race) suggests a class-and-shift texture that Cape-centred fiction lacks.

## 3. Television and film: what reviewers praised and criticised

### Takeaway
Prestige crime series since 2019 are mostly international co-productions led by a traumatised profiler or detective hunting a serial or child killer; reviewers praise performances, cinematography and local specificity, and criticise gruesomeness and, in the sharpest pieces, the "cops versus evil" frame itself. The titles praised for doing something different centre people outside the police: a house cleaner (*Unseen*), a small-time blackmailer (*Number 37*), compromised cops with no moral high ground (*iNumber Number*, *Wild Is the Wind*), or a returning outlaw (*Five Fingers for Marseilles*).

### Inferences
- The export model (co-production, imported lead or showrunner, serial killer, traumatised female profiler) is now the cliché on screen: *Reyka*, *Catch Me a Killer*, *Cape Town*, *Zulu* and *Recipes* all cast a non-South African or internationally known lead. A game with a local, unglamorous protagonist and no serial killer would read as a deliberate break.
- Repeated praise goes to specificity of place and to protagonists with no institutional power. Repeated complaints are about gore, dead and abused women and children as the engine of plot, and moral binaries.
- McMichael's critique gives a concrete brief: show how local power (employers, councillors, police, contractors, churches) is networked, instead of isolating one monstrous individual.

## 4. True crime and podcasts

### Takeaway
South African true crime splits into (a) investigative-journalist books that treat one murder as a window onto state-underworld collusion (Wiener's *Killing Kebble*, Pauw), (b) streaming docuseries built on a charismatic detective or a lurid case (*Devilsdorp*, *Rosemary's Hitlist*, *Senzo*), and (c) podcasts (*Alibi*, *True Crime South Africa*). The recurring criticisms are that it turns grief into entertainment, adds nothing new, and over-represents some victims.

### Cited Findings

- Mandy Wiener, *Killing Kebble: An Underworld Exposed* (2011), on the 2005 shooting of mining magnate Brett Kebble. Wiener: the murder "was this weak point in the organised crime syndicates' operations. It is that one instance that exposed everything else ... they had the police commissioner on the take and then Kebble died and this blew it all up." She draws the same pattern for Cyril Beeka and Lolly Jackson exposing Radovan Krejcir's links to senior police. The article notes it is a murder "for which no-one will ever be punished". ### Inferences
- The dominant true-crime shape is "one killing cracks open a network". - Real investigators' own accounts (Booysen) are less heroic than screen versions: botched early work, colleagues possibly implicated, no recognition at retirement.

## 5. Recurring tropes: which are worn or harmful, and what alternatives are praised

### Takeaway
Critics most clearly identify as worn: the damaged alcoholic white detective (conceded by Meyer), the all-explaining corrupt minister (de Kock), the dead or abused woman or child as plot engine (Orford's self-critique; reviewers of *Reyka* and *Donkerbos*), "cops versus evil" (McMichael), the farm murder read as racial attack (Haarhoff, Marx Knoetze, Brodie), and occult or muti explanations that displace material causes. Praised alternatives are township-interior point of view, non-police protagonists, complicit investigators, and attention to political economy.

### Cited Findings

- **Damaged alcoholic white detective.** Griessel is introduced as "an alcoholic" with "family problems"; Mat Joubert in *Cape Town* drinks and contemplates suicide; *Zulu*'s Brian Epkeen is "a broke, barely functioning alcoholic"; *Devil's Peak* (TV) is sold on its "troubled detective". Meyer has himself called Griessel "a bit of a cliché". [WIKI] — [Devil's Peak (novel)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devil%27s_Peak_(novel)); [Cape Town (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Town_(TV_series)); [Zulu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zulu_(2013_film)); [Devil's Peak (TV series)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devil%27s_Peak_(TV_series)); [EXTRACT for Meyer's remark, link uncertain] — [blog, Nov 2012](https://mickmal1.blogspot.com/2012/11)
- A German crime reviewer, Gunter Blank, complained to Mike Nicol that the genre worldwide keeps "chewing up the same ol', same ol' ... serial killers, people with troubled childhoods, old Nazi criminals, heists gone awry". [FULL] — [Daily Maverick, 2 Mar 2012](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2012-03-02-murder-she-wrote-margie-orford-sas-crime-fiction-queen/)
- **Corrupt Black official / rotten state.** De Kock: in Nicol and Smith the crime always traces to "state corruption, usually ministerial", embodied in "wonderfully wicked characters like Sheemina February (Nicol) and Inja Mazibuko (Smith)"; "it can't be quite as bad as Smith makes out". Naidu and le Roux (per reviewer): the genre is "ideologically ambiguous" because white writers set out to expose the "rottenness" of the post-apartheid state. [FULL] — [Daily Maverick / SLiP](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2012-01-23-sa-crime-thrillers-and-the-genre-snob-debate/); [EXTRACT] — [Project MUSE review](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/742413/pdf)
- **Township as backdrop.** De Kock's praise of Mzobe turns on point of view: events are "crystallisations of township conditions", not illustrations of a thesis about the state. Makholwa is described as writing "from within the world of the young, new black middle class". [FULL] — [Daily Maverick / SLiP](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2012-01-23-sa-crime-thrillers-and-the-genre-snob-debate/); [EXTRACT, thesis; link mapping approximate] — [University of Pretoria repository](https://repository.up.ac.za/bitstreams/1fa72dbf-38b1-4af0-ac6e-f0b0078b787f/download)
- **Farm murder.** Mandisa Haarhoff, "Staging a farm murder: the imagined Black perpetrator in Karin Brynard's *Weeping Waters*" (2025), argues that racialising these murders as Black-on-white attacks with genocidal intent is a political campaign, though the murders are real. Hannelie Marx Knoetze argues that nostalgic "Boer" imagery plus "white genocide" claims work to restore white hegemony. A further article reads Darrell Roodt's film *Treurgrond* (2015) as casting Afrikaners as beleaguered and unwelcome. [EXTRACT] — [Penn State research portal](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/staging-a-farm-murder-the-imagined-black-perpetrator-in-karin-bry/); [Journal of Literary Studies](https://unisapressjournals.co.za/index.php/jls/article/view/13120); [Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/750573/summary)
- **Muti and occult killings.** Used in Michael Stanley's *Deadly Harvest*, Beukes's *Zoo City*, McCall Smith's *No. 1 Ladies' Detective Agency* and Mda's *Ways of Dying*. Buntu Makhedama argues *Zoo City*'s muti murder should be read as "faith-based magical realism", plausible within Bantu cosmology, where the others keep realist conventions. The Wits commentary warns that occult explanations mask "race, power and precarity". Booysen's memoir deliberately strips the supernatural from the Krugersdorp killers. [EXTRACT] — [Imbizo journal](https://unisapressjournals.co.za/index.php/Imbizo/article/view/17153); [UNISA thesis record](https://ir.unisa.ac.za/handle/10500/31686); [EWN](https://www.ewn.co.za/2025/09/15/satanic-panic-5-occult-crimes-that-gripped-south-africa-s-imagination); [FULL] — [Daily Maverick](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2022-06-15-the-devilsdorp-files-an-unflinching-true-crime-account-of-the-appointment-murders/)
- **Foreign crime boss.** Recurs in fiction (a Colombian drug dealer in *Devil's Peak*) and in true crime (Krejcir and "Eastern European syndicates" in Wiener's account). [WIKI] — [Devil's Peak (novel)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devil%27s_Peak_(novel)); [FULL] — [Daily Maverick](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2011-04-11-killing-kebble-mandy-wieners-murderous-best-seller/)
- **Investigative journalist or profiler as hero.** Many leading crime writers are ex-journalists (Meyer, Orford, Nicol, Mzobe, Makholwa, McClure, Brynard); protagonists include a journalist-profiler (Clare Hart), a former journalist (Zinzi December in *Zoo City*), a PR writer (Makholwa's Lucy), and the journalist-narrator of *Alibi* and *Devilsdorp*. Orford on why she left journalism for fiction: facts could not reach a truth that is "ambiguous, complex and three dimensional". [FULL] — [Daily Maverick, 2 Mar 2012](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2012-03-02-murder-she-wrote-margie-orford-sas-crime-fiction-queen/); [WIKI] — [Zoo City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoo_City)
- **Violence against women as subject and spectacle.** Orford called her five Hart novels "a kind of portrait of organised misogyny" and traced the violence to apartheid-era "masculine licence" that "sublimated into the body, into the family, and into intimate spaces"; she later dropped the series because the profiler "was reproducing the male gaze". [EXTRACT] — [Johannesburg Review of Books, 8 Sep 2022](https://johannesburgreviewofbooks.com/2022/09/08/what-happens-when-you-have-a-crime-committed-against-a-person-that-never-ever-comes-to-an-end-margie-orford-talks-to-jennifer-malec-about-her-new-novel-the-eye-of-the-beholder/); [FULL] — [Daily Maverick 2012](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2012-03-02-murder-she-wrote-margie-orford-sas-crime-fiction-queen/); [Daily Maverick 2022](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2022-11-23-margie-orford-focuses-our-gaze-on-secrets-obsession-and-intergenerational-trauma-and-produces-an-exquisite-work-of-art/)
- **"New South Africa" disillusion arc.** De Kock describes Smith's world as one where "nothing and no one in South Africa is 'clean' anymore" and compares its ANC hitmen directly to "the corrupt, murdering Nat functionaries under PW Botha". Makholwa's *Red Ink* was praised for the opposite: "no old South African baggage". [FULL] — [Daily Maverick / SLiP](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2012-01-23-sa-crime-thrillers-and-the-genre-snob-debate/); [WIKI] — [Angela Makholwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angela_Makholwa)
- **Detective partnership across race.** The template is McClure's Kramer and Zondi (1971 to 1991). It recurs in Hart and Faizal, in *Zulu*'s Sokhela and Epkeen, in *Donkerbos*'s Fanie and Tsedza, and in Meyer's Griessel, Cupido and Kaleni. A scholar quoted by The Conversation says it is "just about impossible" to write a South African crime novel without exploring race and "the racial politics of the transforming South African Police Services". [WIKI] — [Kramer and Zondi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kramer_and_Zondi); [EXTRACT] — [The Conversation, 2020](https://theconversation.com/african-crime-and-detective-fiction-reshapes-the-genre-135142)
- **Alternatives praised.** Non-police protagonists whom society does not value (*Unseen*); morally compromised cops with no clean option (*iNumber Number*, *Wild Is the Wind*); political-economy readings of rural violence (McMichael on *Reyka*); criminals described as criminals, not monsters or witches (Booysen); Mzobe's coming-of-age-through-crime structure. [WIKI] — [Unseen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unseen_(TV_series)); [Avenged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avenged_(2013_South_African_film)); [EXTRACT] — [Africa Is a Country](https://africasacountry.com/2022/11/kwazulu-natal-gothic)

### Inferences
- The cross-racial partnership began (in McClure) as a device for showing who really does the detecting while another man takes the credit. In later uses it tends to become a reconciliation emblem. The original, sharper use (unequal credit, the junior partner's access to information through domestic and street networks, a public mask over private respect) is the version worth learning from.
- A coal-town game is exposed to four ready-made clichés: the corrupt municipal or Eskom-linked official as sole villain, the hostel or informal settlement as scenery, a muti angle for colour, and a white ex-cop protagonist with a bottle. Each has a documented critique above and a documented alternative.
- No critic I found says corruption should be left out; de Kock's objection is to corruption as a total explanation that removes local cause and individual choice.

### Gaps
- I did not find a critic who names "the corrupt Black official" as a racial stereotype in those words; the cited critiques concern totalising state rottenness and white authorship. The stronger framing would be the writer's inference.
- No direct critical discussion of the "foreign crime boss" as xenophobic trope was found, though xenophobia is listed as a theme in Mhlongo's work.
- The alcoholic-detective critique rests on Meyer's own remark (search extract, link uncertain) and on pattern evidence; I found no South African critic's essay devoted to it.

## 6. Language and voice: multilingual dialogue and translation

### Takeaway
The successful model in print is English narration seasoned with retained Afrikaans (and some Xhosa or Zulu) words, often with a glossary; on screen, the films critics single out are shot largely in African languages or Afrikaans with subtitles, not in English.

### Cited Findings

- Meyer writes in Afrikaans; K. L. Seegers's English translations keep Afrikaans words. Kirkus on *The Dark Flood*: enough Afrikaans is kept "to add flavor, though readers will sometimes wonder what they mean" (examples: *laaitie*, *stompie*). Publishers Weekly on *Cobra*: "Afrikaans expressions season the story" and a glossary is included. [EXTRACT] — [Kirkus](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/deon-meyer/the-dark-flood/); [Publishers Weekly](https://publishersweekly.com/9780802123244)
- World Literature Today, "Desegregating Language: The New Afrikaans Crime Novel": in the Afrikaans originals a reader may find "entire pages of dialogue in English, with only the reporting verbs left in Afrikaans"; Seegers kept the Afrikaans *ou* and rendered *niks* with the Spanish *nada* to signal mixed speech. [EXTRACT] — [World Literature Today](https://worldliteraturetoday.org/node/4722)
- Meyer gives Vaughn Cupido a distinct Cape Flats idiolect, noted in a South African review. [EXTRACT] — [Tydskrif vir Letterkunde](https://letterkunde.africa/article/view/1607/1354)
- A North-West University thesis compares strategies for translating culture-specific items in Meyer (direct versus via English, into German). [EXTRACT] — [NWU repository](https://repository.nwu.ac.za/bitstreams/29f23df1-2b62-4e1c-9051-8cf9dd33cf8a/download)
- Brynard's *Weeping Waters* was translated from Afrikaans by Maya Fowler and Isobel Dixon. [EXTRACT] — [Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9781609454463)
- Orford was praised for "perfectly rendered local colour and lingo" (William Saunderson-Meyer). [FULL] — [Daily Maverick, 2 Mar 2012](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2012-03-02-murder-she-wrote-margie-orford-sas-crime-fiction-queen/)
- Mhlongo writes "in Soweto dialect". [WIKI] — [Wikipedia: Niq Mhlongo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niq_Mhlongo)
- In McClure, Zondi's value to Kramer is partly linguistic: his "incisive deductions and translations". [WIKI] — [Kramer and Zondi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kramer_and_Zondi)
- Screen language choices: *iNumber Number* is in Zulu and tsotsitaal; *Five Fingers for Marseilles* in isiXhosa, Sesotho, English and Afrikaans (Sesotho title *Menoana e Mehlano ea Marseilles*); *Number 37* in Afrikaans with English subtitles; *Silverton Siege* in English, Afrikaans and Zulu; *Heart of the Hunter* in English and isiXhosa; *Gaia* in English and Afrikaans; *Ludik* is Netflix's first Afrikaans series, subtitled and dubbed in five languages. By contrast *Reyka*, *Unseen*, *Catch Me a Killer* and *Blood & Water* are listed as English. [WIKI] — [Avenged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avenged_(2013_South_African_film)); [Five Fingers for Marseilles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Fingers_for_Marseilles); [Number 37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_37_(film)); [Silverton Siege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silverton_Siege); [Heart of the Hunter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_of_the_Hunter_(film)); [EXTRACT] — [Slashfilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/five-fingers-for-marseilles-review); [Netflix](https://about.netflix.com/news/netflixs-first-afrikaans-title-ludik-coming-soon)
- News24's *Ludik* review enjoyed its "very funny Africanisms" (sic, as extracted). [EXTRACT] — [News24](https://www.news24.com/life/arts-and-entertainment/tv/reviews/ludik-20220825)

### Inferences
- Two working techniques for an English-language game: (1) keep specific untranslated words with context or an optional glossary, and (2) mark code-switching structurally (who switches into which language with whom) instead of spelling accents phonetically.
- Language can be an investigative mechanic, as it is for Zondi: what a character can learn depends on which languages they speak and who will talk to them in which register.
- Wikipedia's "English" label for series such as *Reyka* probably understates on-screen code-switching; do not infer from it that the dialogue is monolingual.

### Gaps
- No source found on how Highveld or Mpumalanga speech (isiZulu, siSwati, isiNdebele, Sesotho, Afrikaans, fanakalo on mines, tsotsitaal/iscamtho) has been handled in crime fiction. This appears to be open territory and would need a language consultant.
- No critique found of glossaries or italics as exoticising, though that debate exists in postcolonial criticism generally.
- The World Literature Today essay was read as an extract only.

## 7. Noir and hard-boiled adaptation, including Red Harvest and African noir

### Takeaway
South African hard-boiled writing begins with Black Drum writers in the 1950s, not with the post-1994 boom; Nicol and Smith are the main contemporary noir stylists. I found no South African work that explicitly adapts *Red Harvest* or its town-tamer structure; the nearest structural relative is the western *Five Fingers for Marseilles*.

### Cited Findings

- Tyler Scott Ball, "Sof'town Sleuths: The Hard-Boiled Genre Goes to Jo'burg" (Cambridge): places the Drum generation "within the orbit of the American hard-boiled genre"; staff writers Arthur Maimane and Mbokotwane Manqupu "developed South Africa's first hard-boiled detectives". [EXTRACT] — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/469489505EEB8E14D4B4D9651039DBBC)
- Maimane, writing as Arthur Mogale, ran a long series about a detective called "the Chief", "in the style of American crime writers like Raymond Chandler". [EXTRACT] — [South African History Online](https://sahistory.org.za/people/arthur-maimane)
- Marta Fossati: Maimane's narrator O. Chester Morena is a Black private detective in Johannesburg's underworld; a Black detective who solves crimes and carries a legal gun was subversive under apartheid, so the hard-boiled style "went beyond mere entertainment". She also treats H. I. E. Dhlomo as a precursor. [EXTRACT] — [Il Tolomeo, 2021](https://doi.org/10.30687/tol/2499-5975/2021/23/018)
- Mike Nicol is reported to see Hammett and Chandler as "inexorably enmeshed into the DNA" of the present South African form; the Mail & Guardian called the style of his trilogy "hard-boiled" or "hard core". [EXTRACT] — [LitNet on *Of Cops & Robbers*](https://www.litnet.co.za/staring-down-the-barrel-mike-nicols-of-cops-and-robbers/); [Mail & Guardian, 2009](https://mg.co.za/friday/2009-10-23-eminently-exportable/)
- De Kock on Smith: "The style is noir"; the endings are "a kind of karmic inevitability of mutual self-destruction, the regular operators going down alongside the hyper-corrupt politicos in an orgy of revenge and counter-revenge". [FULL] — [Daily Maverick / SLiP](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2012-01-23-sa-crime-thrillers-and-the-genre-snob-debate/)
- De Kock's list of the genre toolkit: "hardboiled noir style, thriller pace, stereotypical or sensational presentation of character and scene, the fascination of hyper-evil, horror, and explicit violence". [FULL] — [same article](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2012-01-23-sa-crime-thrillers-and-the-genre-snob-debate/)
- *Joburg Noir* (2020) belongs to the city-noir anthology format launched by Akashic Books in 2004; Jacana frames it around memory and identity as much as crime. [EXTRACT] — [Brittle Paper](https://brittlepaper.com/2020/10/joburg-noir-paints-a-complex-portrait-of-one-of-africas-beloved-cities/); [Jacana](https://jacana.co.za/product/joburg-noir/)
- *Zoo City*: a noir-inflected missing-person plot led by an ex-journalist and recovering addict in an alternate Hillbrow; won the 2011 Arthur C. Clarke Award. [WIKI] — [Wikipedia: Zoo City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoo_City)
- *Five Fingers for Marseilles*: an outlaw returns to a small town now under a new predatory power and must decide whether to fight; read by Cinema Escapist as an allegory of colonialism's damage. A returning-stranger western, not a play-both-sides plot. [WIKI + EXTRACT] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Fingers_for_Marseilles); [Cinema Escapist](https://www.cinemaescapist.com/?p=5539)
- An Indian outlet headlined a South African film "Neo-Noir From South Africa Gets to the Heart of Social Violence"; title only, film not identified. [EXTRACT, title only] — [LiveWire](https://livewire.thewire.in/?p=47175)
- African comparison: Quartey (Ghana) and Michael Stanley (Botswana) write police procedurals, not noir; The Conversation has a survey piece, "African crime and detective fiction reshapes the genre". [WIKI] — [Kwei Quartey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwei_Quartey); [EXTRACT] — [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/african-crime-and-detective-fiction-reshapes-the-genre-135142)

### Inferences
- A *Red Harvest*-shaped story (an outsider sets a company town's factions against each other) would be new in South African crime fiction as far as this search shows. Its closest local cousins, Smith's mutual-destruction endings and the "town owned by one man" mining thrillers, have been criticised for nihilism and weak sense of place, which marks the risks.
- The Drum precedent gives a local, Black-authored hard-boiled lineage to draw on in place of importing Chandler directly: the private detective as a figure of illegitimate, improvised authority under a hostile state.
- De Kock's toolkit list doubles as a checklist of what to ration: "hyper-evil" and "sensational presentation of character" are where he sees genre overpowering accuracy.

### Gaps
- No source mentions Hammett or *Red Harvest* in connection with a specific South African work. Searches found only the general Nicol remark. Treat "no SA adaptation exists" as unproven.
- African noir more broadly (Nigerian, Kenyan, Francophone *polar*) was not researched beyond the two procedural authors; out of time budget.
- Ball's and Fossati's articles were read as abstracts and extracts only.

## 8. Games and interactive fiction

### Takeaway
I found no released detective or crime game set in a recognisable South African place. South African studios have made a comedy-noir cat detective game (*Nine Noir Lives*), a post-apocalyptic South African adventure (*Beautiful Desolation*), a heist game about repatriating artefacts (*Relooted*), and at least one unreleased or obscure tech-noir RPG (*Blackbay Blues*). A grounded detective game set in a real South African town appears to be unoccupied ground.

### Cited Findings

- South Africa has "over 60" domestic studios, most with fewer than five staff; notable titles listed include *Stasis* (2015), *Semblance* (2018) and *Nine Noir Lives* (2022). [WIKI] — [Wikipedia: Video games in South Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_games_in_South_Africa)
- *Nine Noir Lives* (Silvernode Games, Pretoria; PC/Mac, September 2022): a "point-and-lick" comedy-noir adventure with feline PI Cuddles Nutterbutter in the fictional city of Meow Meow Furrington; Steam user reviews "Very Positive" (114 reviews when searched); OpenCritic 67% of critics recommend. Not set in South Africa. [EXTRACT] — [Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/914890); [Game Developer press release](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/press-release/point-click-and-lick-to-find-the-purrpetrator-in-nine-noir-lives-available-now-on-pc)
- *Blackbay Blues*: described by its Cape Town solo developer as a near-future tech-noir RPG with a private investigator in fictional Riverbay City. Release status unknown. [EXTRACT] — [ModDB](https://www.moddb.com/games/blackbay-blues)
- *Beautiful Desolation* (The Brotherhood): isometric point-and-click adventure "set in a post-apocalyptic version of South Africa". Science fiction, not crime. [WIKI] — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beautiful_Desolation)
- *Relooted* (Nyamakop, Johannesburg): a heist game about taking African artefacts back from Western museums; drew international coverage. [EXTRACT] — [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/gaming/rescue-african-artifacts-from-colonizers-museums-in-the-heist-game-relooted-000035161.html); [OkayAfrica](https://www.okayafrica.com/relooted-african-artifact-repatriation-video-game/)
- *Among the Innocent: A Stricken Tale* (zero degrees games, Johannesburg): first-person adventure, first of five planned chapters; genre and setting not confirmed. [EXTRACT] — [RAWG](https://rawg.io/games/among-the-innocent-a-stricken-tale)
- Vice ran a feature on making a game in South Africa "while rationing water and amidst rolling blackouts". [EXTRACT, title only] — [Vice](https://vice.com/en/article/how-to-make-a-video-game-while-rationing-water-and-amidst-rolling-blackouts)

### Inferences
- The gap in games mirrors the gap in fiction: nobody has made a grounded detective game in a real South African town, and nobody has set a crime story in the coal Highveld. The project sits on both gaps at once.
- *Relooted*'s reception suggests international press will engage with a South African game that has a clear political premise and local authorship.

### Gaps
- Interactive fiction (Twine, itch.io, mobile narrative) set in South Africa was not searched in depth; small titles are likely missed.
- I did not verify whether *Among the Innocent* (said by other sources to be set on a Free State farm) has crime elements. [UNVERIFIED]
- No South African reception or criticism of foreign detective games (for example *Disco Elysium*) was gathered; a South African critical view of that game's handling of class and a post-revolutionary police force would be directly relevant and is worth a follow-up.
